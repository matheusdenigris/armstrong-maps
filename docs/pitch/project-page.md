## High-level project summary

Armstrong Maps is a layered map of Mars for planning and following a walk on the surface, made for
a phone. The crew picks a landing site on a 3D globe, stacks real NASA data layers (imagery,
topography, thermal and spectral maps, weather and radiation), taps the stops of a route and gets a
safety verdict at once: go, caution or no-go, with the reasons. The astronaut follows the same
plan in a helmet view with heading, next waypoint, life support, radiation dose and a round map.
Safety comes first: every number shown has a source, and everything simulated is labelled as
simulated.

## Detailed project description

**What it does**

- **Globe.** A 3D Mars textured with the Viking colour mosaic. Blue outlines mark where NASA
  publishes high-resolution mosaics; five landing sites (Gale, Jezero, Meridiani, Gusev, Elysium)
  lead down to the surface.
- **Surface map.** Optical, topographic, thermal and spectral layers from NASA's Mars Trek, down to
  25 cm per pixel with HiRISE, with the real traverses of Curiosity and Perseverance and a scale
  bar measured on Mars.
- **Safety check.** For each route the app computes distance, time outside, life support used,
  the farthest point from the base and the radiation dose, and returns go, caution or no-go with
  the reasons.
- **The walk, from two seats.** The ship console starts a simulated walk; the astronaut's visor
  shows a real rover panorama, a heading tape, life support, dose and a round map turned so that
  ahead is up. Pausing in one view pauses the other.
- **Phone first.** Built with React Native and Expo for Android and iOS, and exported to the web.

**How it works**

React Native with Expo and Expo Router; three.js for the globe. Mars Trek tiles are
equirectangular, not Web Mercator, so the app computes the visible tiles and the projection
itself. Every distance uses the radius of Mars (3,389.5 km) in tested TypeScript, never an Earth
formula from a map library.

**Why it matters**

On Mars a walk is limited by the suit, not by the terrain alone. Bringing the data and the limits
into one view lets the crew see the risk before stepping outside, and lets the astronaut keep the
plan in sight while walking.

**Limits we are open about**

- The route is a straight line between waypoints: slopes and obstacles need an elevation model and
  are planned next.
- The astronaut's position and the walk clock are simulated.
- The suit limits (2.5 km/h, 8 h of life support with a 20 percent reserve, a 5 km walk-back limit,
  20 min per stop) are assumptions of this project, not NASA rules.
- Radiation is the average surface dose published from Curiosity's RAD; the weather is measured at
  Gale only.
- The visor photograph is a real panorama, but its framing does not follow the real heading.

## Use of artificial intelligence

The code, tests and documentation were written with Claude (Anthropic) through Claude Code, under
the team's direction; Claude also found and tested the NASA data sources. The product idea is the
team's. No image, map, sound or scientific data was generated by AI: all of it comes from NASA and
its partners, credited in the app.

## NASA data

- Mars Trek WMTS (NASA/JPL): Viking MDIM 2.1, MGS MOLA with Mars Express HRSC, Mars Odyssey THEMIS,
  MGS TES, MRO CTX and HiRISE.
- Curiosity and Perseverance traverses (mars.nasa.gov).
- Curiosity REMS weather feed.
- MSL RAD surface dose (Hassler et al. 2014, Science 343).
- NASA Image and Video Library: rover panoramas PIA25176 and PIA24264.
